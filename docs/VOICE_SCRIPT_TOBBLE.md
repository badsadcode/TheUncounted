# Old Tobble's tales: voice script

Old Tobble stands by the well in Bellwether. Ask him for a story and pick one: a window opens and the tale's text scrolls slowly while his recording plays, timed so the scrolling ends with the voice. Without a recording, it scrolls at reading pace.

**Files:** save each tale as `assets/audio/voice/<file name>.mp3` (or `.ogg` / `.wav`), using the file name below. One file per tale. Any length is fine (the scroll follows it); these run about 1 to 1.5 minutes read at a storyteller's pace.

**Voice:** an old village storyteller, warm and rambling, delighted to have an audience, completely serious about complete nonsense. Pauses before the punchlines. (ElevenLabs tags like [chuckles] or [whispers] are fine in your copy of the text; the on-screen text can stay as it is.)

Also nice to record (optional): his greeting `dlg.tobble.root` and barks `bark.old_tobble.1`–`4`.


## 1. The Winter the River Froze

**File:** `tale.tobble.1`

Now. This was the winter the river froze. You won't remember it. Nobody remembers it. That's how cold it was: it froze the remembering.

The whole village went down to the river, and there it was, white and hard as a bishop's heart. So we skated. Every day for a month. Old Marta's grandmother did a pirouette so beautiful the crows applauded. The miller skated to Saltmere and back before breakfast and was never the same again. I taught a pig to skate. Not well. But enthusiastically.

And then, one morning, the sun came out, and the river began to flap.

Flap, I said. Rivers don't flap. And it turned out it wasn't the river at all. It was Mrs. Pennyworth's washing. She'd laid out every sheet she owned to dry, and the frost had got them, and we'd been skating on her bedlinen for a month.

She was very cross. Very cross Mrs. Pennyworth. She made us all wash it again.

We never did find the river. I think it left. Can't say I blame it.


## 2. Duke, the Goat Who Could Count to Four

**File:** `tale.tobble.2`

When I was a lad, I had a goat. His name was Duke, and Duke could count to four.

Not three. Not five. Four. You'd hold up four turnips and Duke would stamp four times. Clever as a magistrate. You'd hold up three turnips and Duke would just look at you. Disappointed, like. As if you'd let the both of you down.

So I entered him in the county fair. The Grand Arithmetical Livestock Competition. There was a sheep that could nod yes. A pig that could spell 'pig', almost. And a turnip. Just a turnip. Its owner said it was a very good listener.

Duke stamped to four in front of the whole county. Perfect. The crowd wept. And then the judges gave first prize to the turnip.

The turnip couldn't count at all! It couldn't do anything! It was a turnip!

Turned out the turnip's owner was the judge's brother-in-law.

Politics, that was. Duke never stamped again. He took up the flute. Badly. But with feeling.


## 3. The Edge of the World

**File:** `tale.tobble.3`

I once walked all the way to the edge of the world. People say you can't. People say it's too far, or it isn't there, or there are dragons. Well, I went.

You go out past Saltmere. Past the last pier, past the last fish, past the last fisherman who'll admit to being one. Then it's second on the left, by the rock that looks like Oakhaven if you squint. Then straight on for three days. Bring a sandwich.

And there it is. The edge of the world. And do you know what's there?

A fence.

Just an ordinary fence. Wooden. Bit wobbly. And somebody, long ago, had nailed a sign to it, and the sign said, in big careful letters: NO.

That's all. Just: NO.

Well, I thought about it. I thought about it for a good long while. And then I turned around and came home.

Good sign, that. Clear. Doesn't waste your time. I wish more signs were like it. I wish more people were like it.


## 4. The King Who Was Afraid of Tuesdays

**File:** `tale.tobble.4`

Long ago, before the Great Silence, before the Bureau, before soup, there lived a king who was afraid of Tuesdays.

Nobody knew why. Some said a Tuesday had been rude to him as a boy. Some said he'd been born on one and never got over it. But every Monday night, regular as the tide, the king would go to bed and sleep straight through to Wednesday morning.

And the kingdom carried on without him. Taxes on Tuesdays were voluntary. Weddings on Tuesdays didn't count. A whole army once invaded on a Tuesday, found nobody to surrender to, and went home confused.

He ruled for forty years, that king. Forty years, and never once saw a Tuesday.

And then, one week, he woke up early. Just a little bit early. He opened his eyes, and it was dark, and he didn't know what day it was. And he lay there, trembling, until the sun came up.

It was a Thursday. He'd overslept the Wednesday as well.

Died on the spot. Of surprise.


## 5. Why the Well Is Here

**File:** `tale.tobble.5`

This well. You've been looking at it. Everyone does. Wondering why it's here, right in the middle of everything.

Well. I don't know either.

It was here when I came, and I came before the village. Before the road. Before the Bureau put up its first sign telling everyone where they were and what they owed. There was just me, and a field, and this well. Already here. Already old.

I asked it, once. I leaned right over and said: well, well, why are you here? And it said nothing. Wells are like that. Very discreet.

So I tell it things instead. Everything, really. Fifty years of things. My secrets. Other people's secrets. What I really think about the Bureau. The thing about Mrs. Pennyworth's washing.

And the well keeps them. Every one. Down there in the dark, all the things this village ever whispered.

Sometimes, late at night, if you put your ear to the stones, you can hear them. All the secrets. Talking to each other.

They're mostly about me. I don't mind. It's nice to be thought of.


## 6. The Dragon and the Pie

**File:** `tale.tobble.6`

There was a dragon, once. Came to Bellwether. Huge thing, red as a bad sunset, and it landed right there in the square and roared, and it said: GOLD. BRING ME ALL YOUR GOLD.

Well. We didn't have any gold. This is Bellwether. We had turnips, and opinions, and a lot of rain.

So Marta's grandmother, who was afraid of nothing except draughts, walked straight up to that dragon with a pie. Rabbit and onion. Still warm. And she held it up and said: you look tired, love. Have you eaten?

The dragon looked at the pie. The dragon looked at Marta's grandmother. And then it ate the pie, very carefully, in one bite, and a single tear rolled down its great scaly cheek.

And it flew away, and we never saw it again.

We argued for years afterwards about why. Some said it was the best pie Marta's grandmother ever made. Some said it was the worst.

Hard to tell, with dragons. They're very private about their feelings.


## 7. How I Invented the Chair

**File:** `tale.tobble.7`

Did you know I invented the chair?

It's true. Before me, there were no chairs. None. Everybody just sort of hovered. You'd go to a feast and everyone would be hovering round the table, knees bent, arms out, sweating. Exhausting. Nobody lived past forty. Their legs gave out.

And one day I thought: what if the hovering was done by a thing, instead of by you?

I built it out of four sticks and a plank and a great deal of hope. I sat on it. And I tell you, the whole village came running. People wept. A man proposed to it.

So I went to the Bureau to patent it. Filled in Form Twelve: Inventions, Domestic. Stamped. Signed. Witnessed by a goat.

And they lost it.

So now everyone in the kingdom sits for free, and I get nothing. Not a penny.

Next time you sit down, you'll feel it. A little twinge. Somewhere about the conscience.

That's guilt, that is. That's me.


## 8. My Cousin the Skeleton

**File:** `tale.tobble.8`

Once, in my younger days, I fought a skeleton.

Down in the crypt, it was. I'd gone looking for my hat. Don't ask why my hat was in the crypt. It was a different time. And out of the dark comes this skeleton, rattling and clacking, and it squares up to me, and we fight.

For three days and three nights we fought. It hit me with a femur. I hit it with a candlestick. It hit me with my own hat. Terrible business.

And on the fourth morning, we were both so tired we just sat down. Side by side on a coffin. And it looked at me, and I looked at it, and I said: you've got my grandfather's chin.

And it had! It was my cousin Albert! Been dead a hundred years and never told anyone!

Lovely fellow, Albert. Bit thin. We still write to each other, you know.

Well. I write. He mostly rattles. But you can tell he means well.
